2. Understanding vs. faith

In the first line, Anselm refers to God as, ``you who grant understanding to faith.’’ In characterizing God in that way, what is Anselm saying about God? What would it mean to, ``grant understanding to faith’’?

Faith suggests believing without evidence, or at least without proof. Understanding implies knowing the reasons why.

To grant understanding to faith would be to supply the reasons why, so that one is no longer believing just on faith.

Anselm is suggesting that God has the power to do this. To give him insight into why it must be true that God exists.

What is Anselm asking for when he asks God to grant that, ``insofar as you know it is useful for me, I may understand that you exist as we believe you exist, and that you are what we believe you to be’’?

Anselm is asking God to reveal to him the reasons why it must be true that God exists. He is asking for proof.

3. God as the greatest thing that can be thought of

Anselm says, ``Now we believe that you are something than which nothing greater can be thought.’’ What are some examples of things that can be thought of?

There are things that really exist that can be thought of, like the Eiffel Tower. There are also things that do not exist that can be thought of, like a fire-breathing dragon.

Are some of the things that can be thought of ``greater’’ than others?

It’s perhaps a bit awkward or strange to say that some of the things which can be thought of are ``greater than’’ others. But that’s also not an entirely non-sensical or unreasonable idea either.

What might a thing, ``than which nothing greater can be thought,’’ be like?

A thing, ``than which nothing greater can be thought’’ would presumably have lots of awesome qualities, like being wholly good, all-powerful, and all-knowing.

Might there be multiple different things, ``than which nothing greater can be thought’’?

This would mean that there are some ties atop the scale of greatness. This doesn’t seem entirely impossible.

But let’s ignore this complication for now. In that case, we can speak of the greatest thing that can be thought of.

And we can take Anselm to be saying, not just that God is something than which nothing greater can be thought, but that God is the thing than which nothing greater can be thought. Because there is only one such thing.

So when Anselm says, ``you are something than which nothing greater can be thought,’’ let’s take him to be saying that God is the greatest thing that can be thought of.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of

Then the plan for the rest of the argument is to show that the greatest thing that can be thought of must really exist.

Are there some really amazing things that can be thought of that do not really exist?

Clearly, yes, e.g., a fountain of youth, or a spring that heals loved ones or brings them back from the dead.

So the idea that the greatest thing that can be thought of really exists is quite extraordinary.

You might think that as we contemplate greater and greater things, they get more and more fantastical, and it gets harder for such things to exist.

But Anselm is saying that the absolute greatest thing that can be imagined really exists. How lucky we are then!

4. Existing in the understanding vs existing in reality

Why does Anselm say that the fool, ``surely understands what he hears’’ when he hears me say, ``something than which nothing greater can be thought’’?

Because the fool doesn’t respond with utter confusion. Instead, he denies that God exists. He denies that the greatest thing that can be thought of is real.

If he did not even understand what was being discussed, he would claim confusion--``I don’t know what you’re talking about’’--rather than denying that God exists.

At the end of the lines we just read, Anselm says, ``and what he understands exists in his understanding, even if he does not understand that it exists [in reality].’’ He elaborates on that distinction in the next few lines. So now let’s read the next three sentences. [``For it is one thing...’’]

So what distinction is Anselm drawing when he says, ``For it is one thing for an object to exist in the understanding and quite another to understand that the object exists in reality.’’ How would you explain it in your own words?

If something exists in my understanding, then that just means that I understand it. I can think about it. If I “understand that such and such exists,” that means that I realize or know that it exists in reality. I might think about something, and so it exists in my understanding, in Anselm’s terminology, without acknowledging or being willing to concede that it really and truly exists.

Now let’s read the last sentence of the current paragraph. [``So even the fool must admit...’’]

What does Anselm take himself to have demonstrated by this point of the argument?

That the greatest thing that can be thought of at least exists ``in the understanding.’’ There is still a long way to go from there to the conclusion that the greatest thing that can be thought of really exists.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding.

5. Anselm’s key claim

According to Anselm, you must admit that the greatest thing that can be thought of ``exists in the understanding.’’ He next goes on to say, ``And surely that than which a greater cannot be thought cannot exist only in the understanding.’’ What does Anselm mean by saying that such a being, ``cannot exist only in the understanding’’? What is the other option?

Existing in reality. So Anselm is here saying that the greatest thing that can be thought of cannot just exist in the understanding. It has to exist in reality too. This is a huge claim. It would get Anselm where he wants to go.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. ... (N) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. So:(N+1) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N)] So:(N+2) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+1)]

So let’s try to figure out how Anselm argues for this key claim that the greatest thing that can be thought of cannot just exist in the understanding.

6. Filling out the argument for Anselm’s key claim

Let’s read the rest of this last paragraph. [``For if it exists only in the understanding...’’]

Select a student at random to read. That’s a lot to process. Let’s try to dig into it one piece at a time.

What does the word `for’ indicate at the start of the second sentence?

That Anselm is about to give reasons for what he just stated previously. You can read `for’ as, ``And that’s true for the following reasons...’’ So what follows are the reasons for the crucial claim he has just made.

The third sentence starts with `So...’. What does that tell us?

That what follows is being drawn as a conclusion. And what is it being drawn as a conclusion from? The previous line.

So we have this claim, ``if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater.’’ And then what, in Anselm’s words, is being drawn as a conclusion from that?

The conclusion that Anselm draws is, ``if that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater cannot be thought is that than which a greater can be thought.’’

So let’s now fill out the outline of Anselm’s argument a bit more. What should we put after (2)?

We can add (3) and (4), drawn as a conclusion from (3):

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3) ``If it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater.'' So:(4) ``If that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater *cannot* be thought is that than which a greater *can* be thought.'' [from (3)] ... (N) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. So:(N+1) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N)] So:(N+2) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+1)]

Next Anselm says, ``But that is clearly impossible.’’ What is it that Anselm is here saying is impossible?

It’s impossible to think of something greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of.

Let’s now add that claim to the argument:

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3) ``If it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater.'' So:(4) ``If that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater *cannot* be thought is that than which a greater *can* be thought.'' [from (3)] (5) We cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. (N) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. So:(N+1) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N)] So:(N+2) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+1)]

Now, line (4) is an `if..., then...’ statement. Does anyone remember what we call statements like that?

Conditional statements, or just conditionals.

Conditionals have what we call an `antecedent’ (the `if’ part of the statement), and what we call a `consequent’ (the `then’ part of the statement).

For line (4), the antecedent is, ``that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding.’’ Let’s label that A.

And the consequent is, ``that than which a greater cannot be thought is that than which a greater can be thought.’’ Let’s label that B.

What’s the relationship between statement B, and line (5)?

(5) denies what B asserts. In other words, (5) is Not B.

And what follows from if A, then B, and Not B?

Not A. That is modus tollens reasoning.

And what is Not A, in words?

That that than which a greater cannot be thought does not exist only in the understanding.

And does that claim appear in our outline of Anselm’s argument? Is there a line in the argument, as we have reconstructed it so far, that denies the possiblity of the greatest thing that can be thought existing only in the understanding?

Yes, line (N). So line (N) is Not A.

So line (N) follows from (4) and (5).

So let’s relabel the argument as we have it so far:

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3) ``If it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater.'' So:(4) ``If that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater *cannot* be thought is that than which a greater *can* be thought.'' [from (3)] (5) We cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So:(6) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (4),(5)] So:(7) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(6)] So:(8) God exists in reality. [from (1),(7)]

7. The move from (3) to (4)

So the key claim (6), which gets Anselm to his ultimate conclusion, is drawn validly, via moduls tollens, from (4) and (5).

(5) seems unobjectionable. So what about (4)? It’s saying that if the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it.

Why should we accept that? Anselm draws it as a conclusion from (3). So now we have to turn our attention to (3). The key question becomes whether there any interpretation of (3) on which it is both true and it entails (4).

(3) says, ``if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater.’’ Let’s focus on the, ``which is greater’’ part of that statement. What is Anselm saying there? What is greater than what, on his view?

The idea seems to be that existing in reality as well as the understanding is greater than existing in the understanding alone. In other words, existing in reality adds to a thing’s greatness.

So part of what line (3) seems to be saying is that existing in reality adds to a thing’s greatness. Let’s break that out separately from the other part of line (3).

With the other part of line (3) being, “if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well.’’

We’ll label those parts (3.1) and (3.2) respectively.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3.1) Existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. (3.2) ``If it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well.'' So:(4) ``If that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater *cannot* be thought is that than which a greater *can* be thought.'' [from (3.1),(3.2)] (5) We cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So:(6) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (4),(5)] So:(7) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(6)] So:(8) God exists in reality. [from (1),(7)]

8. Is the argument sound?

Is this a sound argument? If not, why not?

A key thing to focus on is claim (3.2), that if a thing exists only in the understanding, ``it can be thought to exist in reality as well.’’

What does that mean?

On one reading, probably the most natural one, Anselm is saying that even if something exists only in your mind (``only in the understanding’’), you can still imagine that it exists in reality. That gives us this:

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3.1) Existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. (3.2a) If a thing exists only in the understanding, you can still imagine that it exists in reality. So:(4) If the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it. [from (3.1),(3.2a)] (5) We cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So:(6) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (4),(5)] So:(7) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(6)] So:(8) God exists in reality. [from (1),(7)]

Reading (3.2) as (3.2a), it seems true.

However, on this reading of (3.2), the inference from (3.1) and (3.2) to (4) is not valid.

To see that, we have to go back to a point we made earlier: that you can think of things that do not really exist, like fire-breathing dragons, and you can also think of things that really do exist, like the Eiffel Tower.

When you are thinking of something that does not really exist, like a fire-breathing dragon, you can imagine it really existing. That still doesn’t mean that you are thinking of something that really exists, like the Eiffel Tower. You are just thinking about something that does not exist, but imagining or pretending that it does exist.

Assuming that (3.1) is true---that existing in reality adds to a thing’s greatness--what that means is that when there is a really existing thing that I am thinking of, like the Eiffel Tower, I am thinking of something greater than what I would be thinking of if the thing in question were just a figment of my imagination.

So, for example, imagine the people who designed the Eiffel Tower. And compare them thinking about it in the design phase, and them thinking about it once it’s finished. Only in the second case is there a really existing Eiffel Tower that they are thinking about. Premise (3.1) implies that they are then thinking about something greater than what they were thinking about in the design phase, before the Eiffel Tower was actually constructed.

But if you’re thinking about something and you are just imagining that it really exists--there isn’t a really existing version that you are thinking about, you are just pretending that it exists---then (3.1) does not imply that you are thinking of something greater.

So if we read (3.2) in the first way we have described, as (3.2a), then it’s true, but then (3.1) and (3.2) do not entail (4).

Alternatively, we could take (3.2) to be saying that if something exists only in the understanding, then the there is a really existing version of it that you could think about it. That would give us this:

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3.1) Existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. (3.2b) Whenever a thing exists only in the understanding, there is a really existing version of it that you can think about instead. So:(4) If the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it. [from (3.1),(3.2b)] (5) We cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So:(6) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (4),(5)] So:(7) The greatst thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(6)] So:(8) God exists in reality. [from (1),(7)]

On this way of reading (3.2), the inference from (3.1) and (3.2) to (4) is valid. Because if there was always a really existing version to think about instead, then (3.1) would imply that you could always think of something greater.

However, (3.2b) is clearly false, and indeed it’s arguably contradictory---if a thing exists only in your understanding, then it doesn’t really make sense to talk about a “version” of it that also exists in reality. If it exists in reality, then it doesn’t only exist in your understanding in the first place.
