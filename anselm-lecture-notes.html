Anselm's Ontological Argument

Philosophy — Lecture notes

Here we have this argument from Anselm. It's meant to show that God exists. Our goal is to figure out whether this argument really does prove what it is trying to prove. And, if not, why not.

The argument is contained within a relatively short passage. So let's just start from the beginning of the passage and work our way through.

In the first line, Anselm refers to God as, "you who grant understanding to faith." In characterizing God in that way, what is Anselm saying about God? What would it mean to, "grant understanding to faith"?

Faith suggests believing without evidence, or at least without proof. Understanding implies knowing the reasons why. To grant understanding to faith would be to supply the reasons why, so that one is no longer believing just on faith.

Anselm is suggesting that God has the power to do this. To give him insight into why it must be true that God exists.

What is Anselm asking for when he asks God to grant that, "insofar as you know it is useful for me, I may understand that you exist as we believe you exist, and that you are what we believe you to be"?

Anselm is asking God to reveal to him the reasons why it must be true that God exists. He is asking for proof.

Anselm says, "Now we believe that you are something than which nothing greater can be thought." What are some examples of things that can be thought of?

There are things that really exist that can be thought of, like the Eiffel Tower. There are also things that do not exist that can be thought of, like a fire-breathing dragon.

Are some of the things that can be thought of "greater" than others?

It's perhaps a bit awkward or strange to say that some of the things which can be thought of are "greater than" others. But that's also not an entirely non-sensical or unreasonable idea either.

What might a thing, "than which nothing greater can be thought," be like?

A thing, "than which nothing greater can be thought" would presumably have lots of awesome qualities, like being wholly good, all-powerful, and all-knowing.

Might there be multiple different things, "than which nothing greater can be thought"?

This would mean that there are some ties atop the scale of greatness. This doesn't seem entirely impossible. But let's ignore this complication for now. In that case, we can speak of the greatest thing that can be thought of. And we can take Anselm to be saying, not just that God is something than which nothing greater can be thought, but that God is the thing than which nothing greater can be thought — because there is only one such thing. So when Anselm says, "you are something than which nothing greater can be thought," let's take him to be saying that God is the greatest thing that can be thought of.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of

Then the plan for the rest of the argument is to show that the greatest thing that can be thought of must really exist.

Are there some really amazing things that can be thought of that do not really exist?

Clearly, yes — e.g., a fountain of youth, or a spring that heals loved ones or brings them back from the dead. So the idea that the greatest thing that can be thought of really exists is quite extraordinary. You might think that as we contemplate greater and greater things, they get more and more fantastical, and it gets harder for such things to exist. But Anselm is saying that the absolute greatest thing that can be imagined really exists. How lucky we are then!

Why does Anselm say that the fool, "surely understands what he hears" when he hears me say, "something than which nothing greater can be thought"?

Because the fool doesn't respond with utter confusion. Instead, he denies that God exists — he denies that the greatest thing that can be thought of is real. If he did not even understand what was being discussed, he would claim confusion — "I don't know what you're talking about" — rather than denying that God exists.

So what distinction is Anselm drawing when he says, "For it is one thing for an object to exist in the understanding and quite another to understand that the object exists in reality." How would you explain it in your own words?

If something exists in my understanding, then that just means that I understand it — I can think about it. If I "understand that such and such exists," that means that I realize or know that it exists in reality. I might think about something, and so it exists in my understanding, in Anselm's terminology, without acknowledging or being willing to concede that it really and truly exists.

What does Anselm take himself to have demonstrated by this point of the argument?

That the greatest thing that can be thought of at least exists "in the understanding." There is still a long way to go from there to the conclusion that the greatest thing that can be thought of really exists.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding.

According to Anselm, you must admit that the greatest thing that can be thought of "exists in the understanding." He next goes on to say, "And surely that than which a greater cannot be thought cannot exist only in the understanding." What does Anselm mean by saying that such a being "cannot exist only in the understanding"? What is the other option?

Existing in reality. So Anselm is here saying that the greatest thing that can be thought of cannot just exist in the understanding — it has to exist in reality too. But why? This is a huge claim. It would get Anselm where he wants to go.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. ... (N) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. So: (N+1) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N)] So: (N+2) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+1)]

So let's try to figure out how Anselm argues for this key claim that the greatest thing that can be thought of cannot just exist in the understanding.

Select a student at random to read. That's a lot to process — let's try to dig into it one piece at a time.

Let's first focus on the part where Anselm says, "So if that than which a greater cannot be thought exists only in the understanding, then that than which a greater cannot be thought is that than which a greater can be thought. But that is clearly impossible." What is it that Anselm is here saying is impossible?

It's impossible to think of something greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of.

And what, according to Anselm, would lead to that impossible conclusion?

That impossible conclusion would follow, according to Anselm, if the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding — meaning, it existed in the understanding but not in reality. So now we can fill out a little more of Anselm's reasoning.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. ... (N) If the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it. (N+1) But, just by definition, we cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So: (N+2) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (N),(N+1)] So: (N+3) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N+2)] So: (N+4) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+3)]

But, according to Anselm, why is it true that if the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it? From where is Anselm getting that?

He's drawing that — if the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, we could think of something greater than it — as a conclusion from what he says in the previous line, because he says, "So if that than which a greater cannot be thought..." So whatever is being said in the previous line, it should be stuff that somehow implies line (N) above.

Let's now, then, focus on the line where Anselm writes, "For if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater." What is "greater" than what, according to Anselm?

Existing in reality as well as the understanding is greater than existing in the understanding alone. In other words, existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. This is part of what Anselm is relying on in drawing line (N). So we could add it to the argument.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3) Existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. ... (N) If the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it. (N+1) But, just by definition, we cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So: (N+2) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (N),(N+1)] So: (N+3) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(N+2)] So: (N+4) God exists in reality. [from (1),(N+3)]

The other thing that Anselm says here, from which he is drawing his crucial conclusion, is, "if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well." So we could add that to the argument.

(1) God is the greatest thing that can be thought of. (2) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in the understanding. (3) Existing in reality adds to a thing's greatness. (4) "If a thing exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well." So: (5) If the greatest thing that can be thought of existed only in the understanding, then we could think of something greater than it. [from (3),(4)] (6) But, just by definition, we cannot think of anything greater than the greatest thing that can be thought of. So: (7) The greatest thing that can be thought of cannot exist only in the understanding. [from (5),(6)] So: (8) The greatest thing that can be thought of exists in reality. [from (2),(7)] So: (9) God exists in reality. [from (1),(8)]

What does Anselm mean by saying, "if it exists only in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well"?

It's not clear what he means.

On one reading, perhaps the most natural one, he is saying that if something exists only in your mind, you can still imagine that it exists in reality. That seems true. However, on that reading of (4), it does not combine with (3) to entail (5) — because imagining that a thing exists in reality is different from there being a really existing version of that thing that you are then imagining or thinking of. In the latter case it would follow from (3) that you are thinking of something greater than what you were originally thinking of.

On another reading of (4), Anselm is saying that if something exists only in your mind, there is a really existing version of it that you can also think about. Then it would follow from (3) that whenever something exists only in your mind, you can think of something greater than it. But, on this reading, the claim is clearly false — indeed it's arguably contradictory: if a thing exists only in your mind, then there can't be a "version" of it that also exists in reality.
