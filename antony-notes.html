1. What Antony is arguing for

What is the main conclusion that Antony argues for in her article?

That God does not exist.

People mean different things by God. Any particular version of God that she is arguing against?

An eternal, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good being.

Note that her language is a little bit hedged when she states her conclusion. Bottom of 37, “I’m going to argue in this essay that in the end, the atheists are right: the problem of evil...constitutes a strong enough argument against theist to justify atheism. But I want also to show that the matter is a little more complicated thatn some atheists realize.

2. The basic idea of the argument

Before we get to the step-by-step breakdown of her argument, what’s the basic idea? What’s the basic problem for people who claim that God exists?

The existence of suffering.

Not evil per se, but suffering of all kinds.

And why is suffering a problem?

Because God could stop it (all-powerful), and someone who cared would do that. And yet we suffer.

3. An initial reconstruction

Antony offers an initial step-by-step reconstruction of the argument that she deems the Logical Argument from Suffering. What does she think of the argument in this form?

That it is not sound.

And what does Antony think is wrong with the argument in this form?

The first premise is false.

And why is the first premise wrong?

Sometimes morally good beings do allow suffering that they could prevent.

What example does Antony give to show that the first premise is not correct?

She gives the parent-child example. Parent lets toy get ruined in the rain in order to teach child a valuable lesson.

4. A second reconstruction

And is the first premise just hopeless in Antony’s view? Or is there a modified version of it that works?

It can be modified slightly to make it acceptable.

And what’s the modification she suggests making to the first premise?

She turns it into the principle of no tolerance unless:

(1*) No morally good being would allow suffering if he or she were able to prevent it *unless* he or she had a *good reason* to permit it.

She points out that just replacing the first premise with this modified version still does not give us a sound argument though. Why not?

Because now line (3) does not follow from the first two premises.

An extra assumption is needed.

What assumption does Antony suggest adding to the argument?

She proposes adding the premise (2.5), No Good Reason, which states:

(2.5) There is no good reason that a morally good, omnipotent being could have to allow suffering.

5. The problem with the second attempt

With these two modifications in place, what does Antony now think of the argument?

She’s still negative about it. 40: “The atheist should concede that the Logical Argument is a failure.”

And what’s the problem with the current version of the argument, according to Antony?

The added premise (2.5) is not absolutely certain (it’s not a conceptual or logical truth?).

6. The final form of the argument. Is its conclusion still important?

Antony then puts forward a last modification to the argument, and calls the resulting argument--it’s final form--the Evidential Argument from Suffering. How is it different from the previous version?

It concludes merely that probably there is no God, based on the premise that probably there is no good reason that a morally good, omnipotent being could have to allow suffering.

So it reaches a weaker conclusion, but from a premise that is now easier to defend.

How weak is the conclusion that this final version of the argument reaches? Is it still a significant conclusion?

Antony herself suggests that it’s still a very significant conclusion. In fact, it’s not really that different from the original conclusion.

She says that the atheist, ``can’t show that the existence of suffering proves that there is no God, but perhaps she can show that the existence of suffering provides very good evidence that there is no God.’’

In a footnote, she comments:

This may seem like a substantial concession, but it isn’t. In every discipline besides mathematics, probability is sufficient. Physicists and chemists do not establish their results with perfect certainty. They show that they are highly probable given the evidence. If the atheist can show that the nonexistence of God is highly probable in the same sense in which the basic facts of chemistry are highly probable, the atheist wins.

How would you explain in your own words what Antony is saying here? Do you agree with her?

7. What makes for a good reason?

Is this final version of the argument sound? As she starts her defense of it, Antony says, ``The atheist should start by explaining what it is for someone to have a `good reason’ for permitting suffering.’’ Why should the atheist do this? How would it help them?

The key premise in their argument says that there is not a good reason for suffering. This premise can be bolstered by describing what a good reason would be, and then showing that nothing like that exists.

To get at what makes for a good reason, Antony goes back to her parent-child example, where parent did indeed have a good reason. She says:

In our story, Parent’s reason for allowing Child to suffer was that Parent wanted to teach Child an important lesson, and allowing her to experience the painful consequences of her own negligence was the only way to do it. What makes this a good reason?

How does Antony answer her own question here?

First is that the parent acted on the basis of a morally laudable goal.

Second is that it was a sufficiently important morally laudable goal. Couldn’t let child suffer just to not interrupt a story.

Third is that the parent’s action was fair---it was directed towards ultimately helping the child themselves, as opposed to someone else.

8. What are some candidate good reasons for the suffering we have on Earth? Do they meet the criteria?

Is there, then, any good reason for the suffering that exists? What might the theist say in response to that?

Antony considers one thing they might say:

So the first requirement that would have to be met for God to have a good reason for permitting the amount of suffering we observe in the world is that that reason would have to be very, very, very important, because we re talking about an enormous amount of suffering...Well, how about this as a surpassing important moral goal: eternal bliss?’’

What’s being suggested here by the theist?

That we suffer because it’s necessary to get in to Heaven?

And what is an issue with that answer from the theist?

Why is it necessary for us to suffer to get there?

43: “It’s very difficult to see what kind of `necessity’ could force God to trade off suffering now for greater happiness later.”

We have limited means to accomplish our goals of greater happiness. No choice but to go through suffering to get there. But God is all-powerful.

Next Antony considers the so-called free will defense. What is that?

God allows us to suffer because it’s necessary for maintainig our free will.

And what is Antony’s problem with the free-will defense?

Plenty of suffering not due to human choices. So it could be prevented without hampering our free will.

(Also, couldn’t God have just made us kindler, gentler souls? Or created a world with less competition for resources?)

9. Other potential responses?

What else might the theist propose as a reason for all the suffering that exists?

Let students suggest some.

Also could mention this. The textbook raises the following potential response to Antony’s argument:

If God eliminated suffering altogether, human beings would not need one another. Children would not depend on their parents for their basic needs, since God would provide if the parents didn’t. People would not depend on friends and family for care and compassion, since we only need care and compassion because we suffer. God leaves us vulnerable to suffering because some of the most valuable human relationships are only possible if we are vulnerable. God lets us suffer because if he didn’t, love and friendship (which involve care and compassion) would be impossible.

What might Antony’s response to this be?

Antony concludes her article this way:

When we confront seriously the amnout of suffering in the world...and consider the way it is distributed...I think the rational conclusion to draw is that there is no good reason for it. A good reason would have to be one that involved a surpassingly important moral goal, where the achievement of that goal required, in a sense strong enough to constrain an omnipotent being, the mass of suffering we see in the world around us. It’s not enough for the theist to insist that there could be such a reason or to point out that we haven’t proved that there couldn’t be such a reason: the theist has to show what such a reason might be. And if the theist cannot? Then the Evidential Argument from Suffering succeeds.

What do you think of what she says here?

What about the response, “How can we claim to be confident that there is no good reason? Shouldn’t we admit that we just do not know? That it’s beyond our grasp?”

What might the atheist say to this?

Perhaps that if we remain this skeptical, then shouldn’t we be just as open to the possibility that “God” is horrendously evil, and this is the worst of all possible worlds?
