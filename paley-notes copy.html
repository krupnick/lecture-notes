1. Paley’s conclusion

What conclusion is Paley arguing for?

Something like: there exists an intelligent Creator.

Or: life on earth was designed/created by an intelligent Creator.

But also presumably wants to show more than that. That God, as traditionally conceived of by Christians, exists.

What is Paley’s argument for that conclusion? Ultimately, we’ll aim to lay it out in premise-conclusion form. But first let’s look at how he starts.

2. How Paley starts

So how does Paley start? What does he imagine finding on the ground?

A watch and a stone.

And what’s the crucial difference between the two?

The watch serves a purpose/carries out a function via intricate mechanisms. The stone does not.

Pg.20, “When we come to inspect the watch, we perceive (what we could not discover in the stone) that its several parts are framed and put together for a purpose...”

And why does this difference matter?

Beause it means that the watch must have had a designer, whereas the same is not true of the stone.

Point 1: Paley says that the stone might have lain there forever. Here there is a contrast between Paley and someone like Aquinas, who would argue that even the existence of a rock should lead us to posit a Creator.

Point 2: A person who did not know what a watch was might not immediately conclude that it was designed. But they would once they understood what it was doing and how. It could not have come about through, “the operation of causes without design.”

3. What do watches have to do with anything?

Why all this talk about watches? What, according to Paley, is relevantly like a watch in the respect that he has described?

The works of nature---that is, living things. See pg.25, start of Chapter III.

Paley is suggesting that life on earth was also intelligently designed.

We should reach the same conclusion about life on Earth that we do about the watch, and for the same reason. Namely, that there’s no other way to explain the remarkable, “order and contrivance” that we observe in it.

The textbook suggests that the best way to understand Paley’s argument is as an, ``inference to the best explanation.’’ It proposes that we view Paley’s argument as having the following general form:

(1) Some remarkable fact F is observed (2) The best (or perhaps the only) explanation for F is hypothesis H. So:(3) H is (probably) true.

How can we state Paley’s argument so that it fits that general form?

(1) Life on earth exhibits ``apparent design.'' (2) The best explanation for how life came to be that way is that living things are the product of intelligent design. So (3) Living things are the product of intelligent design.

4. An immediate problem and a reply

What’s the most obvious objection to Paley’s argument, especially from our modern, scientifically informed perspective?

That the second premise is false, on the grounds that the theory of evolution offers a better explanation.

Fair enough. Paley’s argument in its original form falls to Darwin. But his argument might be modified to try to get around that objection. The textbook states:

Paley argues that the only reasonable explanation for the observed “design” in nature is that living things were made by an intelligent creator. In this form, the argument was undermine by Darwin. Without appeal to supernatural causes, the theory of evolution by natural selection can explain why living things are well adapted to their environments...

A version of Paley’s argument nonetheless survives the Darwinian response. Darwin’s theory explains why populations of living things change over time. But it does not explain the emergence of life itself. (This is not an objection to Darwin’s theory: it was never meant to explain this process.) Moreover, we can be confident that the first living things exhibited remarkable ``order and contrivance.’’ Any creature capable of reproduction needs intricate systems for taking in food from the environment, copying its genetic material, and so on. So consider the first cell. It is like Paley’s watch--an intricate contrivance that exhibits ``apparent design.’’ Why are its parts so brilliantly adapted to its needs? Modern biology has no answer. So Paley might insist: the only available explanation for this fact is intelligent design. It is reasonable to believe the best explanation. So it is reasonable to believe in intelligent design (28).

So the modified version of the argument refocuses it on to the very first living thigs.

(1) The *very first living things* exhibited ``apparent design.'' (2) The best explanation for how *the first living things* came to be that way is that they were the product of intelligent design. So (3) The first living things were the product of intelligent design.

We can’t object to the second premise of this argument by saying that Darwin’s theory provides a better explanation because the theory of evolution does not address how the very first living things came to exist.

Is there a different scientifc theory that explains how the very first living things came to exist?

Various candidates. But nothing with widespread support. All have holes or flaws.

So if we think the argument is unsound, we have to find a way to object to it without being able to say, “This specific scientific theory offers a better explanation than intelligent design.”

Are there good objections that do not fit into that category?

So if someone comes to you and says that they believe in an intelligent designer God because there is no other way to explain the origin of life, what’s one thing you can say in response?

Why does it have to be a designer God? Even if we concede some design was involved, there are still so many options.

6. Hume’s second objection

What is Hume’s second objection?

(Is there a particular premise in Paley’s argument that this objection denies? If so, on what grounds?)

Can have students just try to answer, or read first, time permitting.

The objection is that the second premise is false---best explanation is intelligent design---because a better explanation is simply, “it was some natural process or other.’’

Byrne writes:

“Paley’s strategy for dismissing no-design alternatives wholesale is to object to the specific evolutionary theories of his day (for instance, that of Erasmus Darwin, Charles’s grandfather). But this is rather like saying that because this apple and that pear are rotten, vegetables are better than fruit. What Paley needs is an argument for choosing the general hypothesis of an unknown designer or designers operating by unknown means over the general hypothesis of an unknown blind process operating by unknown means, and he signally fails to supply one.”

In your own words, what point is Byrne making here?

Showing, of specific scientific theories, that they cannot provide the explanation we are looking for only shows that those theories won’t work.

It doesn’t show that no such theory has a reasonable chance of working.

Byrne writes:

``An example that briefly appears in Darwin’s Black Box nicely illustrates the point. In 2001: A Space Odyssey, a magnetic anomaly in one of the moon’s craters leads to the discovery of a perfectly regular slab buried under lunar soil. The characters have no idea how the slab was constructed, or what it is for, and have never known an artist capable of making one; nevertheless they reasonably conclude that it was designed. But that is precisely because the characters are not in Paley’s position. They know enough about lunar geology, astronomy, and the possibility of extraterrestrial life to discredit the rival hypothesis that the monolith is a natural object (a big crystal, say) that formed on the moon or collided with it.Paley, on the other hand, had no reason, other than the failure of his imagination, to dismiss the hypothesis of `causes without design’.’’

Why are the characters from 2001 not in Paley’s position? What does Byrne mean by that?

He means that they are in a position to say that there is no reasonable no-design explanation available (the same is true for the person who finds a watch and discovers what it does and how).

The characters in 2001 have enough knowledge to conclude that no natural explanation is workable.

By contrast, while Paley, or more modern proponents of ID, may have good reasons for ruling out specific natural/scientific explanations of the phenomena they are pointing to, they do not have good reasons for concluding that no such explanation is workable.
