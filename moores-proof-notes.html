1. The central question before us

We have this argument:

(1) You do not know that the demon hypothesis is wrong. (2) If you do not know that the demon hypothesis is wrong, then you do not know that you are in a classroom, nor that you have hands or a body, that there is an Earth at all, and so on. So: (3) You do not know any of those things.

This argument is either sound or unsound. But which is it?

Ask for show of hands.

2. What to prove to thereby prove the existence of things outside of us

Now we have this article from G.E. Moore. It’s titled “Proof of an External World.”

When we talk about proving that there is an external world, or proving “the existence of things outside of us” as Moore puts it, what does that mean?

What kinds of claims or assertions is one trying to prove when one is trying to prove the existence of things outside of us?

that I have hands; that there are tables and chairs in this room; that there are clouds in the sky; etc.

And what about claims like these: that it seems to me that I have hands; that it looks to me as if there are clouds in the sky; and so on.

Would proving those count?

No. So “proof of an external world” would mean proving things about what the world is really like, not just how it seems.

And if we did prove things like, I really do have hands, there are tables in chairs in this room, there are clounds in the sky, what would that imply about the demon hypothesis?

That it is false.

So if we can prove those things, then we could prove that the demon hypothesis is false.

So Moore’s paper sounds so ambitious, so exciting!

It sounds like he is going to provide us with all the ammunition we need in order to say that line (1) in the demon argument is false.

But for most people Moore’s article turns out to be spectacularly disappointing, at least at first.

3. How Moore purports to prove the existence of things outside of us

The disappointment has to do with how Moore purports to prove the existence of external things.

So let’s read.

It seems to me that, so far from its being true, as Kant declares to be his opinion, that there is only one possible proof of the existence of things outside of us, namely, the one which he has given, I can now give a large number of different proofs, each of which is a perfectly rigorous proof; and that at many other times I have been in a position to give many others (303).

Here Moore references Kant. Kant is his foil, his opposite, in this article.

Moore starts his article with this quote from Kant:

It still remains a scandal to philosophy...that the existence of things outside of us...must be accepted merely on faith, and that, if anyone thinks good to doubt their existence, we are unable to counter his doubts by any satisfactory proof (301).

For Kant, proving the existence of things outside of us is a kind of monumental task, which only he himself as been able to accomplish.

Moore doesn’t think that Kant actually succeded.

I think it is by no means certain that Kant’s proof is satisfactory. I think it is by no means certain that he did succeed in removing once [and] for all the state of affairs which he considered to be a scandal to philosophy. And I think, therefore, that the question whether it is possible to give any satisfactory proof of the point in question still deserves discussion (302).

Now let’s go back to the passage where Moore gives his proof.

It seems to me that, so far from its being true, as Kant declares to be his opinion, that there is only one possible proof of the existence of things outside of us, namely, the one which he has given, I can now give a large number of different proofs, each of which is a perfectly rigorous proof; and that at many other times I have been in a position to give many others. I can prove now, for instance, that two human hands exist. How? By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, “Here is one hand,” and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, “and here is another.” And if, by doing this, I have proved ipso facto the existence of external things, you will all see that I can also do it now in numbers of other ways: there is no need to multiply examples (303).

So what is Moore’s proof of the existence of things outside of us?

(1) Here's one hand, and here's another. So:(2) Two human hands exist.

Moore says that, “there is no need to multiply examples.” But, just to check your understanding, it might be helpful to try producing an example of your own.

What would be an example of another Moore-style proof of the existence of things outside of us?

Here is one leg; Here is another; so two legs exist.

What about one not involving body parts? A little more distant from what Moore literally provides, but still the same idea?

There is a black pen in this bag; there is a red pen in this bag; so there are two pens in this bag; so there exist at least two pens.

4. Why Moore’s proof is disappointing; what a true proof would look like

At this point, most people feel that Moore hasn’t delivered on the promise of his title. He hasn’t really proven the existence of things outside of us.

But why might someone feel that way? What is missing? What more would Moore have to do in order to truly prove the existence of things outside of us?

Prove his premise?

What if Moore then responded by saying: “Ok, no problem. I will prove my premise. What grounds do I have for saying that there’s a hand here? Well, there’s a left hand here. So there’s a hand here. So a human hand exists.”

(1) Here's a left hand. So (2) Here's a hand. So (3) A human hand exists.

Presumably if you were someone who felt that Moore needed to prove his premise, that wouldn’t satisfy you.

But why not? What would Moore have to do to truly prove his premise that here’s a hand?

What if Moore were to argue like this?

(1) It looks to me as if there's a hand here. (2) If it looks to me as if there's a hand here, then there must really be a hand here. So: (3) There's a hand here.

And he could go on to prove (2). Would that work?

Proving the reliability of his perceptual faculties is what is wanted?

Yes

And what if he were to try prove (2) by saying, “I just went to get my vision checked at the doctor’s office, and everything checked out”?

Would that work? If not, why not?

The visit to the doctor’s office could itself be part of an illusion or dream. He can’t just assert that he went there and everything checked out.

So what sorts of claims is Moore allowed to make in his attempt to prove his premise, according to those who want him to do that?

Just claims that he could still trust to be true even if this were a grand illusition.

So claims about how things look or seem to him. (And maybe “self-evident” truths as well).

So, for those who were hoping for something more, a true proof would start just from claims about how things look or appear (plus self-evident truths).

5. Moore’s concessions

Moore concedes that he cannot do what his opponents want him to do. He cannot give an argument that they would regard as a proof of his premise.

[I]f I had proved the propositions which I used as premisses ...then they would perhaps admit that I had proved the existence of external things, but, in the absence of such a proof (which, of course, I have neither given nor attempted to give), they will say that I have not given what they mean by a proof of the existence of external things. In other words, they want a proof what I assert now when I hold up my hands and say, “Here’s one hand and here’s another”...Of course, what they really want is not merely a proof of these two propositions, but something like a general statement as to how any propositions of this sort may be proved. This, of course, I haven’t given; and I do not believe it can be given: if this is what is meant by proof of the existence of external things, I do not believe that any proof of the existence of external things is possible.

When Moore says, “what they really want is not merely a proof of these two propositions, but something like a general statement as to how any propositions of this sort may be proved,” he’s acknowledging that the, “Here’s a left hand; so, here’s a hand” argument wouldn’t work on his opponents.

Note only does Moore concede that he cannot give an argument that his opponents would regard as a proof of his premise, he just concedes outright that he cannot prove his premise.

[Read from “Of course, in some cases...” through rest of paragraph]

Let’s flag that second concession. Not clear it’s the right move. So we will come back to it.

6. “I can know things, which I cannot prove”

Moore concedes that he cannot prove his premise. But he doesn’t think that this prevents it from being the case that he has proven that two human hands exist.

Why not? Because he only needs to prove his premise if doing so is necessary for knowing that premise to be true.

And, he says, proving his premise is not necessary for knowing it to be true.

He says, “I can know things, which I cannot prove; and among things which I certainly did know, even if (as I think) I could not prove them, were the premisses of my two proofs.”

7. Where does this leave us?

So Moore’s final position is: I know my premise (even though I cannot prove it). Therefore, I succeded in proving that two human hands exist. And hence I succeeded in proving the existence of things outside of us.

Is this helpful for responding to the demon argument?

It seems like the response that Moore would recommend is: I know that I have hands. So I know I’m not a BIV, or a non-physical being deceived by an evil demon.

Which doesn’t seem supersatisfying.

It also conflicts with what he says about not being able to prove that he is not dreaming.

8. But should Moore have conceded that he cannot prove his premise?

What if Moore had just conceded that he cannot give an argument which his opponent would regard as a proof of his premise, but then asked: ok, but why is that necessary for proving my premise?

I say, “Here’s a left hand; so here’s a hand,” would be a perfectly good proof of my premise; you say I have to give an argument that traces back to seemings and self-evident truths. Why is that necessary?

You would only think that that was necessary if you thought that all knowledge had to start from there.

But why think that?

The same thought lies behind line (1) of the demon argument.
