1. The central question in Moore’s article

What is the central question that Moore attempts to answer in his article?

Whether it is possible to prove the existence of things outside of us.

See 2nd paragraph pg.302.

[T]here seems to me to be no doubt whatever that it is a matter of some importance and also a matter which falls properly within the province of philosophy, to discuss the question what sort of proof, if any, can given of “the existence of things outside of us.” And to discuss this question was my object when I began to write the present lecture.

And when we talk about proving, “the existence of things outside of us” what does that mean? What does one have to prove in order to prove the existence of things outside of us?

that I have hands; that there are tables and chairs in this room; that there are clouds in the sky; etc.

And what are claims that don’t count as being about “the existence of things outside of us”? Examples of things where proving or establishing them wouldn’t suffice to prove the existence of things outside of us?

that it seems to me that I have hands; that it looks to me as if there are clouds in the sky; and so on.

And what is Kant’s answer to the central question at issue?

Kant’s answer is no, it is not possible. Or at least not possible without an effort of monumental genius. His proof is the only possible proof (third paragraph, pg.302)

What is Moore’s answer to the same question?

Yes, it is possible. In fact, it is quite easy to do so.

2. Moore’s proof

How does Moore purport to prove the existence of external things?

See the first full paragraph, pg.303.

It seems to me that, so far from its being true, as Kant declares to be his opinion, that there is only one possible proof of the existence of things outside of us, namely, the one which he has given, I can now give a large number of different proofs, each of which is a perfectly rigorous proof; and that at many other times I have been in a position to give many others. I can prove now, for instance, that two human hands exist. How? By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, “Here is one hand,” and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, “and here is another.” And if, by doing this, I have proved ipso facto the existence of external things, you will all see that I can also do it now in numbers of other ways: there is no need to multiply examples.

Moore says that, “there is no need to multiply examples.” But, just to check your understanding, it might be helpful to try producing an example of your own.

What would be an example of another Moore-style proof of the existence of things outside of us?

Here is one leg; Here is another; so two legs exist.

What about one not involving body parts? A little more distant from what Moore literally provides, but still the same idea?

There is a black pen in this bag; there is a red pen in this bag; so there are two pens in this bag; so there exist at least two pens.

What is your initial reaction to Moore’s proof? Does he succeed in proving the existence of external things?

3. Moore’s three necessary conditions for giving a proof

Moore writes:

But did I prove just now that two human hands were then in existence? I do want to insist that I did; that the proof which I gave was a perfectly rigorous one; and that it is perhaps impossible to give a better or more rigorous proof of anything whatever.

He goes on:

Of course, it would not have been a proof unless three conditions were satisified.

What are the three conditions that Moore says have to be satisfied in order to successfully give a proof?

the premise is different from the conclusion; he knows the premise; the premise entails the conclusion
