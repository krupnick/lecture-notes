The Argument from Design

PHIL-1 Lecture notes

What conclusion is Paley arguing for? How do you know? Are there particular passages where it becomes clear what conclusion he is trying to establish?

Definitely arguing for the existence of an intelligent Creator. Presumably wants to demonstrate more than that. Last couple of paragraphs contain the most explicit statement of what he is trying to show.

What is Paley’s argument for that conclusion? Ultimately, we’ll aim to lay it out in premise-conclusion form.

Paley imagines finding a stone on the ground, and being asked, ``how the stone came to be there,’’ and then he contrasts that with finding a watch instead, and being asked, ``how the watch happened to be in that place.’’

According to Paley, what answer is not plainly absurd for the stone, but is clearly unacceptable for the watch?

The answer is that it’s lain there forever.

And, according to Paley, ``[W]hy should not this answer serve for the watch, as well as for the stone? Why is it not as admissible in the second case, as in the first?’’

The reason it’s not absurd for the stone, but is for the watch is because the watch, unlike the stone, is such that, ``its several parts are framed and put together for a purpose.’’

According to Paley, assuming that we have observed the watch and understood it, what answer must we give to the question of how the watch came to be? And why?

We must conclude that it was designed and created by an intelligent being. Because there’s no other way to explain its features---it’s ``irreducible complexity’’

And why all this talk about watches? What, according to Paley, is relevantly like a watch in the respect that he has described?

The works of nature---that is, living things. See pg.25, start of Chapter III.

(pg.27--28) The textbook suggests that the best way to understand Paley’s argument is as an, ``inference to the best explanation.’’ It proposes that we view Paley’s argument as having the following general form:

(1) Some remarkable fact F is observed (2) The best (or perhaps the only) explanation for F is hypothesis H. So:(3) H is (probably) true.

How can we state Paley’s argument so that it fits that general form?

(1) Life on earth exhibits ``apparent design.'' (2) The best explanation for how life came to be that way is that living things are the product of intelligent design. So (3) Living things are the product of intelligent design.

prompt: What premise in Paley’s argument is a contemporary, scientifically informed person most likely to object to, and on what grounds?

The second premise, on the grounds that the theory of evolution offers a better explanation.

(pg.28) The textbook states:

A version of Paley’s argument nonetheless survives the Darwinian response. Darwin’s theory explains why populations of living things change over time. But it does not explain the emergence of life itself. (This is not an objection to Darwin’s theory: it was never meant to explain this process.) Moreover, we can be confident that the first living things exhibited remarkable ``order and contrivance.’’ Any creature capable of reproduction needs intricate systems for taking in food from the environment, copying its genetic material, and so on. So consider the first cell. It is like Paley’s watch--an intricate contrivance that exhibits ``apparent design.’’ Why are its parts so brilliantly adapted to its needs? Modern biology has no answer. So Paley might insist: the only available explanation for this fact is intelligent design. It is reasonable to believe the best explanation. So it is reasonable to believe in intelligent design (28).

What is this new version of Paley’s argument? Reconstruct it as another ``inference to the best explanation’’ style argument.

(1) The very first living things exhibited ``apparent design.'' (2) The best explanation for how the first living things came to be that way is that the first living things were the product of intelligent design. So (3) The first living things were the product of intelligent design.

Why does the textbook say that this revised version of Paley’s argument, ``survives the Darwinian response’’?

Because we can no longer object to (2) on the grounds that the theory of evolution by natural selection provides a better explanation.

Alex Byrne says that the Scottish philosopher David Hume---a prominent Enlightenment era figure, writing a few decades before Paley, and thus several decades before Darwin---presented the ``key objections’’ to intelligent design style arguments of the sort that Paley raised. Byrne focuses on two objections from Hume in particular. What is the first objection that Byrne describes? Is there a particular premise in Paley’s argument that this objection denies? If not, what precisely is the objection?

The first objection does not deny either premise, nor does it deny that the reasoning is valid. Instead, it points out that the conclusion falls far short of what the traditional theist would like to establish. It’s compatible with all sorts of views very different from what the theist really wants to say.

What is Hume’s second objection? Is there a particular premise in Paley’s argument that this objection denies? If so, on what grounds?

Yes, the second premise, on the grounds that a better explanation is simply, “it was some natural process or other.’’

Byrne writes:

``In 2001: A Space Odyssey, a magnetic anomaly in one of the moon’s craters leads to the discovery of a perfectly regular slab buried under lunar soil. The characters have no idea how the slab was constructed, or what it is for, and have never known an artist capable of making one; nevertheless they reasonably conclude that it was designed. But that is precisely because the characters are not in Paley’s position.’’

Why are the characters not in Paley’s position? Why is it reasonable for them to conclude that the slab was designed, but it’s not reasonable for Paley, who was writing before Darwin, to conclude that living things were designed?

The characters in 2001 have good reasons for concluding that no natural explanation is workable.

By contrast, while Paley, or more modern proponents of ID, may have good reasons for ruling out specific natural/scientific explanations of the phenomena they are pointing to, they do not have good reasons for concluding that no such explanation is workable.
