## The promise and disappointment of Moore's article

prompt:
We have this argument:
    argument:
    (1) You do not know that the demon hypothesis is wrong.
    (2) If you do not know that the demon hypothesis is wrong, then you do not know that you are in a classroom, nor that you have hands or a body, that there is an Earth at all, and so on.
    So: (3) You do not know any of those things.
This argument is either sound or unsound. But which is it?

note:
Ask for show of hands.

prompt:
Now we have this article from G.E. Moore. It's titled "Proof of an External World."

%Just a brief aside about GE Moore. He embodies a certain approach to philosophy. Not it's job to reveal profound truths that the ordinary person doesn't see. Rather, it's job is to defend and explain the common sense, ordinary world view. It's job is to prevent people from slipping into the kind of confusions that make them conclude that time is unreal, or we know nothing, or the greatest of all conceivable beings must exist, etc.

prompt:
When we talk about proving that there is an external world, or proving "the existence of things outside of us" as Moore puts it, what does that mean? 

What kinds of claims or assertions is one trying to prove when one is trying to prove the existence of things outside of us?

note:
that I have hands; that there are tables and chairs in this room; that there are clouds in the sky; etc.

prompt:
And what about claims like these: that it seems to me that I have hands; that it looks to me as if there are clouds in the sky; and so on.

Would proving those count?

note:
No. So "proof of an external world" would mean proving things about what the world is really like, not just how it seems.

prompt:
And if we did prove things like, I really do have hands, there are tables in chairs in this room, there are clounds in the sky, what would that imply about the demon hypothesis?

note:
That it is false.

prompt:
So if we can prove those things, then we could prove that the demon hypothesis is false.

prompt:
So Moore's paper sounds so ambitious, so exciting! 

It sounds like he is going to provide us with all the ammunition we need to cast aside possibilities like the demon hypothesis once and for all.

But for most people the article turns out to be spectacularly disappointing, at least at first. 




## Moore's proof

prompt:
And why is it disappointing? 

Well, how does Moore purport to prove the existence of external things?

note:
See the first full paragraph, pg.303.
    quote:
    It seems to me that, so far from its being true, as Kant declares to be his opinion, that there is only one possible proof of the existence of things outside of us, namely, the one which he has given, I can now give a large number of different proofs, each of which is a perfectly rigorous proof; and that at many other times I have been in a position to give many others. I can prove now, for instance, that two human hands exist. How? By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, "Here is one hand," and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, "and here is another." And if, by doing this, I have proved *ipso facto* the existence of external things, you will all see that I can also do it now in numbers of other ways: there is no need to multiply examples. 

prompt:
Moore says that, "there is no need to multiply examples." But, just to check your understanding, it might be helpful to try producing an example of your own.

What would be an example of another Moore-style proof of the existence of things outside of us?

note:
Here is one leg; Here is another; so two legs exist.

prompt:
What about one not involving body parts? A little more distant from what Moore literally provides, but still the same idea?

note:
There is a black pen in this bag; there is a red pen in this bag; so there are two pens in this bag; so there exist at least two pens.

prompt:
At this point, most people feel that Moore has way underdelivered on the promise of his title.

prompt:
But Moore is no fool. He's aware that his attempted proof will strike many as unsatisfactory. He writes:
    quote: 
    But did I prove just now that two human hands were then in existence? I do want to insist that I did; that the proof which I gave was a perfectly rigorous one; and that it is perhaps impossible to give a better or more rigorous proof of anything whatever.

prompt:
To answer the question of whether he's proven what he says he has proven, we should consider: what counts as a proof?

## What counts as giving a proof? Moore's three conditions

prompt:
Moore says:
    quote:
    Of course, it would not have been a proof unless three conditions were satisified.
What are the three conditions that Moore says have to be satisfied in order to successfully give a proof?

[Read passage]

note:
the premise is different from the conclusion; he knows the premise; the premise entails the conclusion

prompt:
Moore says that, "all these three conditions were in fact satisfied by my proof."

[Read passage]

prompt:
Is anything else needed for giving a proof? Moore writes:
    quote:
    My proof, then, of the existence of things outside of us did satsify three of the conditons necessary for a rigorous proof. Are there any other conditions necessary for a rigorous proof, such that perhaps it did not satisfy one of them?
Maybe, but it really does not seem like it. Meeting the three conditions seems *sufficient* for giving a proof.  

In other cases we seem to regard meeting these conditions as sufficient. 

## But you have to prove your premise first

prompt:
Moore is aware that people still will think that he has massively failed to deliver. He writes:
    quote:
    But now I am perfectly well aware that, in spite of all that I have said, many philosophers will still feel that I have not given any satisfactory proof of the point in question.

prompt:
If you had to say why he hasn't succeeded, what would you say?

note:
Are the three conditions he listed not sufficient? 

If they are, then is one of the conditions not satisfied?

Is it the second condition, that he does not know his premise? 

If he does not know his premise, why not?

prompt:
Let's skip to the last paragraph, because this is where he takes on the most obvious reply.

prompt:
[Read from, "But another reason..." up to "This view that, if I cannot prove..."]

So what is the argument that Moore sees his opponent making against him? What reasons does he see them having for concluding that he did *not* prove that two human hands exist?

note:
    argument:
    (1) If you cannot prove your premise, then you do not know it.
    (2) You cannot prove your premise.
    So: (3) You do not know it.
    (4) If you do not know your premise, then you did not prove that two human hands exist.
    So (5): You did not prove that two human hands exist.

## Moore's reply

prompt:
And what is Moore's reply to this objection?

[Read from, "This view that..." to the end of the paragraph]

What claim in the argument against him does he deny?

note:
Line (1). He concedes line (2) in the previous paragraph (and again the present one). And line (4) he endorses earlier.

prompt:
And on what grounds?

note:
He knows the premise of his argument, but cannot prove it. So we may know things that we cannot prove.

prompt:
What do you think of this response?

note:
Either proof is required and I do not know my premise; or I do know my premise and proof is not required.

Surely the latter is the sensible choice.

## Another possibly reply

prompt:
Come to think of it, why does Moore concede that he cannnot prove, "Here's one hand, and here's another"?

Why not just prove that in the same style that he purports to prove that two human hands exist?

prompt:
For example, why not give the argument "Here's a left hand, so here's one hand; and here's a right hand; so here's another hand."?

Why not then insist, "The premises entail the conclusion; they are different from the conclusion; and I know the premises"?

The idea that knowing requires being able to give a proof doesn't seem sufficient on its own to defeat what Moore has claimed.

The assumption that is really doing the work builds in an idea about where knowledge starts.

It starts from the inside. With how things look or seem to us.
