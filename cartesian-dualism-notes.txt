### How Descartes proposes to answer the question

Here we might see Descartes as telling us how he proposes to figure out what he is, or what features of his are essential to him.

So let's try to break this down a bit.

## Elisabeth's objection to Cartesian Dualism

prompt:
One of the classic objections to Cartesian Dualism was expressed very well by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia.

prompt:
She writes:
    quote:
    I ask you to please tell me how the soul of a human being, (it being only a thinking substance), can determine the bodily spirits, in order to bring about voluntary actions.
In your words, what is Elisabeth asking here?

note:
How can a non-physical thing have any effect on a physical thing?

prompt:
That does seem mysterious. But Elisabeth doesn't quite leave it at that. She doesn't just depend on us to find it mysterious. She tries to say a little something about why that seems impossible.

prompt:
So let's read the next bit of the passage:
    quote:
    For it seems that all determination of movement is made by the impulsion of the thing moved, by the manner in which it is pushed by that which moves it, or else by the particular qualities and shape of the surface of the latter. 
Can you put this in your own words? What is Elisabeth saying here?

note:
In order for one thing to move another thing ("all determination of movement") the first thing has to *push* or *bump up against* the second thing.

prompt:
Elisabeth goes on:
    quote:
    Physical contact is required for the first two conditions, extension for the third.
Here "extension" means "taking up space."

She's saying that for a first thing to push or bump up against a second thing, the first thing has to make physical contact with the second thing. 

So the first thing has to take up space.

prompt:
But:
    quote:
    You entirely exclude the one [extension] from the notion that you have of the soul and the other [physical contact] appeats to me incompatible with an immaterial thing.

But, on Descartes's view, I am a non-physical, immaterial thing. So I don't take up space. So how I can make anything physical thing move?

prompt:
Elisabeth says:
    quote:
    This is why I ask you for a more precise definition of the soul than that you give in your Metaphysics, that is to say, of its substance separate from its action, that is, from thought.
What is Elisabeth asking for here? What does she mean by asking for a definition of, "its [the soul's] substance separate from its action"?

note:
Descartes is always talking about what the soul *does*--it thinks, doubts, understands, etc.

But what is the soul *made of*? We are just told what it is not (not a wind or fire or breath). Not what it is.

## An argument for Cartesian Dualism

### The conclusion

prompt:
Descartes gives a famous argument in Meditation VI for the conclusion, "I am really distinct from my body, and can exist without it."

prompt:
Let's focus on the "I am really distinct from my body" part. 

In your own words, what is another way of saying, "I am distinct from my body"?

note:
Me and my body are different things.

prompt:
If me and my body are different things, then what am I?

Perhaps we are not then forced to answer in the exact same way that Descartes does. 

Perhaps we do not then have to answer "an immaterial thinking thing." 

But the door has certainly been opened to that conclusion.

prompt:
So what is the argument Descartes gives for the conclusion, "I am distinct from my body"?

### The first line

prompt:
Let's start with the first line:
    quote:
    First, I know that everything which I clearly and distinctly understand is capable of being created by God so as to correspond exactly with my understanding of it.

prompt:
*In Descartes's words*, what is it that is capable of being created by God?

note:
"everything which I clearly and distinctly understand"

prompt:
Let's focus on that phrase, "which I clearly and distinctly understand." Because anything that falls under this heading is something that God can create or bring about, according to this first line. 

prompt:
Can you understand something that you don't agree with?

note:
Yes

prompt:
Can you understand what someone is describing or talking about, even if you do not believe in it yourself?

note:
Yes

prompt:
So something that you "clearly and distinctly understand" is not something that you necessarily believe in or think is real. 

But you can at least *understand it*. You can make sense of it.

prompt:
The first line says that God is capable of creating, "everything which I clearly and distinctly understand...so as to correspond exactly with my understanding of it."

So, in your own words, what does that mean?

note:
God *can* create---not has, or will, but *can* create---anything which I can conceive or make sense of.

"So as to correspond exactly with my understanding of it" means: not just some approximation of what I conceive of, but exactly what I conceive of.

prompt:
And what about things that I do *not* "clearly and distinctly understand"? Can God bring such a thing about?

What would be an example? Would have to be an example of something that I *cannot* make sense of, or conceive of.

note:
A round square

prompt:
Can God bring such a thing about?

note:
The first line is officially silent, but presumably the answer is meant to be no.

prompt:
So we start with a premise that says something like:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.

### The next line

prompt:
The passage continues:
    quote:
    Hence the fact I can clearly and distinctly understand one thing apart from another is enough to make me certain that the two things are distinct, since they are capable of being separated, at least by God

#### The conclusion Descartes draws in this line

prompt:
Note the word `hence' that starts off this line. What does that tell us?

note:
That what follows is a conclusion that is being drawn from what came before.

prompt:
And what about the word `since' that appears near the end of this line? What does that tell us?

note:
That what follows is a reason that is being provided for the conclusion that was just stated.

So it's not itself part of the conclusion. 

prompt:
So, *in Descartes's own words*, what is the conclusion that was just reached?

note:
"the fact I can clearly and distinctly understand one thing apart from another is enough to make me certain that the two things are distinct"

prompt:
Let's work on clarifying what that conclusion is. 

If we take `clearly and distinctly understand' to mean `conceive of', then we can rephrase the conclusion Descartes draws as:
    quote:
    The fact that I can conceive of one thing apart from another is enough to make me certain that the two things are distinct.

prompt:
In the phrase, "is enough to make me certain that *the two things* are distinct," what "two things" are being referenced?

note:
The things that he can conceive apart from one another.

prompt:
So we can rephrase the conclusion that Descartes draws as:
    quote:
     The fact I can conceive of one thing x apart from another thing y is enough to make me certain that the first thing x is distinct from the second thing y.

prompt:
Try to rephrase that as an if, then statement. How would it go?

note:
If I can conceive of x apart from y, then x is distinct from y.

prompt:
So we can see the conclusion that Descartes is drawing here as:
    quote:
    If I can conceive of x apart from y, then x is distinct from y.

#### The premises he draws that conclusion from

prompt:
Now, from what premises does Descartes draw that conclusion? 

prompt:
We know that he's partly relying on the first line:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.
Because that's the assertion that he puts forward right before he says, "Hence..."

prompt:
But we also have this, "since they are capable of being separated, at least by God" piece.

So that's another thing he is relying on in drawing the conclusion he has drawn. 

prompt:
When Descartes writes, "since *they* are capable of being separated, at least by God," what does the "they" refer to here?

note:
The things that can be conceived apart from one another

prompt:
So, according to Descartes, if I can conceive of a first thing x apart from a second thing y, then the first thing x could be separated from the second thing y, at least by God.

prompt:
And how do we know that if I can conceive of x apart from y, then x could be separated from y, at least by God?

note:
Because the first premise tells us that *anything* that I can conceive of, God can bring about.

prompt:
So for the start of the argument, we have:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.
    So (2) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then God at least can separate x from y. [from 1]

prompt:
And from there, Descartes draws the conclusion:
    argument:
    So (3) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then x is distinct from y. [from 2]

prompt:
Now what is being assumed in the move from (2) to (3)?

note:
That if God can separate x from y, then x is distinct from y.

prompt:
So let's add that as an extra premise to the reasoning. That would give us:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.
    So (2) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then God at least can separate x from y. [from 1]
    (3) If God can separate x from y, then x is distinct from y.
    So (4) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then x is distinct from y. [from 2,3]


### The rest of the argument

prompt:
Now we have the rest of the passage. But without examining it too closely, let's just ask:

What assertion, when added to line (4), would yield the conclusion that I am distinct from my body?

note:
That I can conceive of myself apart from my body.

Maybe that is what Descartes is saying in the last couple of lines.

prompt:
So in full the argument would run:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.
    So (2) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then God at least can separate x from y. [from 1]
    (3) If God can separate x from y, then x is distinct from y.
    So (4) If I can conceive of x apart from y, then x is distinct from y. [from 2,3]
    (5) I can conceive of myself apart from my body.
    So (6) I am distinct from my body [from 4,5]

prompt:
The argument talks about "conceving of x *apart from* y" or "x being *separated from* y".

How should we understand that talk?

prompt:
One option that makes sense is to take "apart from" and "separate from" to mean "exists without."

So the argument would be:
    argument:
    (1) Anything that I can conceive of, at least God could bring about.
    So (2) If I can conceive of x existing without y, then God at least can bring it about that x exists without y. [from 1]
    (3) If God can bring it about that x exists without y, then x is distinct from y.
    So (4) If I can conceive of x existing without y, then x is distinct from y. [from 2,3]
    (5) I can conceive of myself existing without my body.
    So (6) I am distinct from my body [from 4,5]

### Motivating the argument

prompt:
What do you think of this argument?

prompt:
Premise (3) seems pretty secure. 

If x = y, then not even God could make x exist without y, since that would be to create a scenario in which x exists without x, i.e., in which x exists and x does not exist. And not even God can do that.

prompt:
Premise (1) doesn't seem so unreasonable. 

prompt:
And premise (5) makes a fairly weak claim. It just talks about what is *conceivable*.

Point 1: Do people who believe in Heaven believe in something that is literally incoherent? If not, then a situation where you exist without your body is at least conceivable (even if unlikely).

Point 2: Just the other day I overheard some students talk about what they would like to be reincarnated as. To contemplate such a scenario is to contemplate a scenario where you exist without your body.
