1. A starting example

You go to a big music festival, or sporting event, or a large mall.

You park your car in lot 2 and make a point of writing down where you parked.

You go enjoy the thing and now it’s time to go home. A friend asks if you know where your car is.

You look at your note and say that, yes, you do. It’s in lot 2.

Then someone says, “Well, what if it was stolen and driven away while we were gone? Do you know it wasn’t?”

You concede, “That seems unlikely, but I guess I don’t know that it wasn’t stolen. So I guess I don’t know that the car is in lot 2.”

Here you start out saying, “I know the car is in lot 2,” but by the end you’ve conceded, “I don’t know the car is in lot 2.”

So by the end you’ve taken back what you said earlier, right? You’re now denying the very thing that you ealier asserted. Correct?

Yes, correct.

Not according to the contextualist about knowledge.

It sounds like that is what is happening. But that is not actually what is happening, says the contextualist.

And, they say, once you understand what is really going on, you’ll understand how we should respond to the skeptic.

So what is going on, on the contextualist view?

2. The contextualist story about what is going on in that example

2.1 Sentences vs propositions

To understand their view, we first should talk about the distinction between a sentence and a proposition.

2.1.1 Difference sentences but same proposition

A sentence is a string of words, like: “The bus will arrive at noon,” or, “At 12pm, the bus will be here,” or, “The arrival time for the bus is 12pm.”

Those are all slightly different strings of words.

The first contains the word `noon’ whereas the second does not.

The last one contains the word `time’ whereas neither of the other two do.

These of course are very minor differences. They don’t really matter.

But that’s precisely the point here. Because even though these are different sentences---thinking of a sentence as a specific string of words---there is clearly something they have in common.

What is it that they have in common?

They are all saying the same thing.

Right. They all put forward the same thought or make the same claim. As a philosopher would put it, they all express the same proposition.

So the proposition is the claim that is being asserted or put forward, which is the same in each case.

The sentence is the exact bit of language that is being used, which is slightly different in each case.

So we have different sentences, all expressing the same proposition.

Take a minute to come up with your own example of that.

2.1.2 Same sentence, but different propositions

It can also happen that the same sentence expresses different propositions on different occasions.

For example, let’s say I’m in Oakland, and I say the words, “It’s raining here” and meanwhile someone in Seattle also says the words, “It’s raining here.”

Then the two of us have uttered the same sentence. We said the same string of words.

But we put forward different claims. I made a claim about the weather in Oakland, and they made a claim about the weather in Seattle.

Another example.

On Tuesday I say, “The Wells Fargo is open tomorrow,” and then again on Saturday I say the words, “The Wells Fargo is open tomorrow.”

I said the same words each time. So I uttered the same sentence on each occasion.

But the claim I put foward the first time was different from the claim I put forward the second time.

Take a minute to come up with your own example of the same sentence being used to express different propositions on different occasions.

Some other examples:

“You’re a student” said to Monica, versus said to James.

“Michelle is smart” said while talking about Michelle Obama, versus said while talk about Michelle Kwan.

“That’s ugly” said while pointing to a coffee table, versus said while pointing to a painting.

“Gavin Newsom is the governor of CA” said on 1/1/26, versus said on 1/1/30.

2.1.3 Context plays a role in determining what is said

The first example (it’s raining here) demonstrates that location matters.

The second example (the wells fargo is open tomorrow) demonstrates that time matters.

The third example (you’re a student) demonstrates that audience matters.

The fourth example (michelle is smart) demonstrates that prior remarks in the conversation matter.

The fifth example (that’s ugly) demonstrates that physical gestures matter.

Philosophers sum all of this up by saying that “context” matters to what is said.

That is, the context in which you are speaking---where you are speaking, when you are speaking, who you are speaking to, among other things---helps to determine what claim it is that you are putting forward.

2.2 Contextualism about knowledge

That was all background to help us understand what contextualism about knowledge is saying.

So now let’s look at how Cohen states contextualism.

2.2.1 The amount of justification needed for knowledge varies by context

At the beginning of the section where he introduces contextualism, he writes:

Almost everyone agrees that in order to know p, one must have good evidence to believe p, i.e., evidence that is sufficient to make one justified in believing p. But how justified in believing p does one have to be in order to know p?

Note that Cohen talks about, “how justified one must be in believing p in order to know p.”

So he’s thinking of justification as something that comes in degrees.

You can be extremely well justified, pretty well justified, only somewhat justified, and so on.

And the question he’s asking is about the amount of justification you need in order to have knowledge.

He goes on to say:

According to contextualism, the standards for knowing, i.e., the standards for how justified one must be in believing p in order to know p, depend on the context.

So, according to the contextualist, the answer to the question, “How much justification do you need in order to count as knowing?” is that there is no such thing as the amount of justification you need in order to count as knowing.

The amount of justification you need in order to count as knowing varies from one context to another.

Ok, but what does that mean?

2.2.2 The same knowledge ascription says different things in different contexts

Here is what it means.

Consider a sentence like, “James knows that his car is parked in lot 2.”

The contextualist is claiming---this is their big claim---that sentences like those change in meaning depending on the context in which they are said.

The context in which such a sentence is uttered helps to determine what is even being claimed.

In one context, saying “James knows that his car is parked in lot 2” will imply just that he has pretty good justification for that belief.

In another context, saying, “James knows that his car parked in lot 2,” will imply that he has extremeley good justification for that belief.

So what it means to say, “James knows...” will change from one context to another.

In particular, what you are claiming about James’s level of justification changes.

2.2.3 The salience of error possibilities creates a high standards context

But this raises a question.

If what it means to say, “James knows...” changes from one context to another---if in one context it means only that James has pretty good justification, and in another context it means that he has extremeley good justification---then why is that?

What is it about the first context that makes it a relatively low standards one?

And what is it about the second context that makes it a high standards one?

Cohen addresses this in the section titled, “The Mechanism of Context Shifting.” He writes:

What explains why some contexts have stricter standards? While contextualist theories differ on details, a common thread is that the salience of error possibilities in a context tends to drive up the standards.

What that means is that as soon as someone starts talking about how you could be mistaken---maybe your car get stolen, maybe someone slipped you a drug that’s making you hallucinate, etc.---that changes the context to one with high standards for knowing.

It changes the context to one where saying, “James knows...” is a bold claim which implies that James has very, very good justification.

Whereas, ordinarily, when no one is bothering to raise those sorts of possibilities, the standards are lower. So saying “James knows...” says something less ambitious, that James only has pretty good justification.

2.3 Applying contextualism about knowledge to the example

So, finally, let’s return to the example we started with.

On the face of it, by the end, you have retracted what you earlier asserted.

But the contextualist has a different take on what is going on. What is it?

On their view, something very significant happens in the middle of the story, when the person raises the possibility that your car was stolen.

2.3.1 The starting context is a low standards one, and your claim to know is correct

Before that point, you were in an ordinary context, with relatively low standards for knowing.

So when you initially said, “I know my car is in lot 2,” you weren’t making a particularly bold or ambitious claim. You were implying only that you had pretty good justification for your belief.

So the claim you made then---the proposition you put forward---was correct.

2.3.2 The ending context is a high standards one, and your claim not to know is correct

But once the person raises the possibility that your car was stolen, that has an important impact on the “context” that you are in.

You are now in a context where someone has raised that possibility.

According to the contextualist, that raises the standards for knowing. It means that any claim to know is now a bold assertion, which would imply that you have extremely good justification.

So saying, “I know my car is in lot 2” at this point would be to put forward a different claim from the claim you made earlier.

And you don’t actually have the level of justification that would be required for making that claim true.

So if you were to say at this point, “I know my car is in lot 2,” you would be wrong.

And so when you say, “I do not know my car is in lot 2,” you are correct.

2.3.3 There is no inconsistency here

It sounds like you are contradicting what you said earlier.

But, according to the contextualist, there is no contradiction.

It’s like starting out in Seattle and saying, “It’s raining here,” and then flying to LA and saying, “It’s not raining here.” There is no inconsistency there.

Saying, “I know” the first time and then “I don’t know” the second time is also not inconsistent, because the context changed in between, in a way that changed what it means to say, “I know.”

3. Applying contextualism to skeptical reasoning

Now apply this same diagnosis to what happens when we are confronted with classic skeptical reasoning.

3.1 The contextualist diagnosis

You start out saying, “I know that I’m here in this classroom, I know that I have hands and a body,” and so on.

Then you are presented with a skeptical argument, such as the evil demon argument.

After which you concede, or there is certainly a temptation to concede, “I don’t know...”

What does the contextualist say is going on here?

When you intially say, “I know that I’m here in this classroom, I know that I have hands and a body...” is what you say true, according to the contextualist?

Yes

And why?

Because at that point you are in a low-standards context.

And you have enough justification for your beliefs to meet that standard.

And then what happens when the skeptical argument is presented?

At that point, an “error possibility” is raised. So the context shifts to one where there are much higher standards for knowledge.

So when you say, “I don’t know...” is what you say true, on the contextualist view?

Yes

And why?

Because you don’t have the level of justification for your beliefs that is required to meet the much higher standard that is now in place.

So when you intially say, “I know” you speak truly, and when you say, “I don’t know” you also speak truly.

This only sounds inconsistent. It’s not actually inconsistent because what it means to say, “I know” has shifted in between.

3.2 Noteworthy consequences of the contextualist diagnosis

So when the skeptic lays out their argument and says, “So you don’t really know anything about the external world,” are they correct?

(According to the contextualist)

Yes, in the sense that what they thereby claim, given the context operating at that time, is true.

But there is a very important `but’. Which is that, when you go back to your ordinary life, and you talk about what you and others know, those claims are also true.

Because, given the context operating at that time, what you say then is correct.

So the contextualist can be seen as conceding to the skeptic, while also taking away the bite of that concession.

Our mistake is to think that conceding to the skeptic undermines our ordinary claims to know.

According to the contextualist, it does not.

4. Objections to contextualism

4.1 Semantic blindness

One objection that Cohen himself raises is the “semantic blindness” objection.

The objection is that contextualism postulates that we are ordinarily---before it is pointed out to us---“blind” to the way in which knowledge is context sensitive.

But, the objection goes, speakers of a language are usually not blind to the context sensitivity of their own language, at least not in such a strong and resilient way.

On the first point.

According to the contextualist, my initial statement, “I know I am in classroom, have hands,” etc, does not conflict with my later statement, “I don’t know...”

But we all initially respond as if they conflict.

Even after contextualism is presented to us, it’s hard to shake the sense that the later statement contradicts the earlier one---that it amounts to retracting or taking back the earlier claim.

So, according to the contextualist, we have a kind of persistent blind-spot when it comes to the context-sensitivity of knowledge ascriptions.

But we don’t seem to have the same blind spot when it comes to other ways in which our language is context sensitive.

We immediately see that one person saying “I’m hungry” is not contradicted by a different person who says, “I’m not hungry.”

We immediately see that one person in CA might truthfully say, “It’s raining here,” while another in Texas truthfully says, “It’s not raining here.” There’s no conflict.

So why this one blind-spot?

4.2 Raised standards would seem to predict incorrectly that other knowledge claims would also fail

Go back to the car example.

According to the contextualist, when the person raises the possiblity of theft, that raises the standards.

It’s the context that is now a high-standards one.

But that would seem to imply that pretty much any claim to know would be wrong.

But that does not seem quite right.

It seems like I still might truly say, “I know there is a basketball game tonight” or, “I know that the restaurant is open until 10pm,” and so on.
