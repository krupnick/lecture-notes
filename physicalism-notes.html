1. What is physicalism?

Physicalism, also known as materialism, is the thesis that “the world is wholly physical.”

So, on this view, there are no souls, no ghosts, no spirits, no angels, and so on.

How many of you would consider yourself physicalists in this sense?

It’s a popular view within philosophy. But there are some dissenters.

David Chalmers and Frank Jackson have both made very prominent arguments against physicalism.

Chalmers’s argument has come to be known as the “zombie argument,” and Jackson’s has come to be known as the “knowledge argument.”

Both arguments have to do with consciousness. They both suggest that consciousness is an outlier. It cannot be fully accounted for by physicalism. That is why physicalism is wrong.

Let’s start with Chalmers’s argument.

2. The zombie argument

2.1 The hard problem: why does this process give rise to experience?

Let’s go to section 4. Start with the first paragraph.

[Read first paragraph]

Chalmers asks, “Why should this process gives rise to experience?”

Imagine focusing on a specific process happening in someone’s brain that we have learned is correlated with feeling pain in one’s leg.

Chalmers is asking: Why does that happening in the person’s brain give rise to pain? Is that result absolutely necessary? Why does it not give rise to some other sensation? Or to no sensation all together? Is that not possible?

Chalmers thinks that is possible---same stuff happening physically, but without the feeling of pain---and the thought experiment he now goes on to describe is meant to support that thought.

And if we accept that thought---that the same stuff could be happening physically, without the feeling of pain---then that seems to imply that physicalism is wrong.

2.2 The definition of a zombie

Let’s first go to the thought experiment that is introduced in the next paragraph, which is supposed to support the idea the same physical stuff could be happening without the feeling of pain.

[Read second paragraph].

We need to try to get clear on what a philosophical zombie is supposed to be. Chalmers says:

A philosophical zombie is a being that is atom for atom identical to a conscious being such as you and me, but it is not conscious.

To understand this definition, we need to understand in what sense zombies are supposed to be “not conscious.”

2.2.1 Zombies are not phenomenally conscious

When Chalmers defines a zombie as a being that is “not conscious,” despite being atom for atom identical to a conscious being like you or me, he means that it is not “phenomenally conscious.”

So what does that mean?

Philosophers make a distinction between two different kinds of mental states: those that are “phenomenally conscious” and those that are not.

So, on this use of the term, being phenomenally conscious is a property that some mental states have, but that others lack.

To say that a mental state is a phenomenally conscious mental state is to say that there is “something it is like” to be in that state.

Think of the phenomenally conscious mental states as the ones that you can’t possibly be in during a completely dreamless sleep.

Whereas a mental state that you could still be in even then is not a phenomenally conscious mental state.

Some examples.

phenomenally consciousnot phenomenally conscious
feeling painbelieving that Norton published an intro philosophy text
hearing a banging noiseintending to pay your rent
seeing blueanticipating that it will rain

So a zombie is supposed to be “not conscious” in the sense that it lacks “phenomenal consciousness,” meaning that it does not enter into any phenomenally conscious mental states.

It might still be correct to describe it as entering into other mental states.

So zombies are not by definition completely devoid of any mental life. They are just devoid, by definition, of any phenomenally conscious mental life.

2.2.2 Zombies are otherwise the same as us

Chalmers says:

Unlike the zombies found in Hollywood movies, philosophical zombies look just like normal humans from the outside, and their behavior is indistingushable from that of a conscious being.

Why, based on how Chalmers has defined what a zombie is, would they look just like normal humans?

Because they are atom for atom duplicates of you/me.

So, on the outside, they are the same as us. And what about the inside? When a zombie stubs its toe, what happens on the inside?

The same physical process as happens in us.

So, when your zombie twin stubs its toe, would it hop up and down on its other foot, just like you?

Yes

So its behavior would be the same.

2.3 An initial presentation of the argument

Now we have some sense of what a philosophical zombie is supposed to be.

Chalmers now goes on to say:

There is little reason to think that philosophical zombies really exist. But what matters for our puposes is simply that the idea is coherent. There is no internal contradiction in the idea of a zombie, the way that there is an internal contradiction in the idea of a round square.

Chalmers continues:

The hard problem of consciousness might then be put as the problem: Why are we not zombies?

The same physical stuff that is happening inside of your brain or my brain right now could (Chalmers thinks) be happening without any “conscious experience” accompanying it. That’s what the zombie thought experiment shows.

Yet there is conscious experience going on for you and for me right now.

So why is that? Where does our conscious experience come from? The answer can’t just point to what is going on in us physically. Because all of that could be taking place without any conscious experience.

The answer must go beyond the physical. And thus physicalism is false.

2.4 A step-by-step reconstruction

Let’s slow down and try to reconstruct the argument step by step.

First, let’s introduce a definition. Let’s say that a zombie version of our world would be a world that is physically the same as our world actually is, but which is completely devoid of phenomenal consciousness.

The first step in Chalmers’s argument is that we can coherently imagine such a world.

(1) A zombie version of our world is conceivable.

The next step is to deduce from there that a zombie version of our world is possible---meaning that the world could have turned out that way, i.e., physically the same, but without phenomenal consciousness.

(1) A zombie version of our world is conceivable. So: (2) A zombie version of our world is possible, i.e., the world could have turned out physically the same, but without phenomenal consciousness. [from 1]

But if physicalism is true, then our world is wholly physical. There is nothing here beyond the physical.

So, according to the physicalist, any world that is exactly the same as ours physically wouldn’t be missing anything that is here.

So if physicalism is true, the world could not have turned out physically the same, but yet lacking something that our world has.

(1) A zombie version of our world is conceivable. So: (2) A zombie version of our world is possible, i.e., the world could have turned out physically the same, but without phenomenal consciousness. [from 1] (3) If physicalism is true, then our world is wholly physical. (4) If our world is wholly physical, then it could not have turned out physically the same, yet lacking something that our world has. So: (5) If physicalism is true, the world could not have turned out physically the same, yet lacking something that our world has. [from 3,4]

And our world does have phenomenal consciousness. So if physicalism is true, the world could not have turned out physically the same, yet lacking phenomenal consciousness.

(1) A zombie version of our world is conceivable. So: (2) A zombie version of our world is possible, i.e., the world could have turned out physically the same, but without phenomenal consciousness. (4) If our world is wholly physical, then it could not have turned out physically the same, yet lacking something that our world has. But then from lines (2) and (7), it follows that physicalism is false.
